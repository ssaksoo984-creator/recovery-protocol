# Me, and How I Work
### — Soomin Park

---

## 0. Who I Am

I'm a designer, and I also act, write, and direct. These might look like separate
pursuits, but they share one thing in common — empathizing, expressing, and
connecting. The way I try to understand a character's emotions on stage isn't
different from the way I try to understand what a user wants in front of a screen.

That instinct got sharper as I moved through very different kinds of
organizations — startups, ad agencies, freelance work, public-sector projects.
Each one asked for something different. Some prioritized speed; others required
strict adherence to manuals and documentation. Some systems were built for
internal staff, others for the public. Some interfaces needed to grab attention;
others needed to feel calm and stable. Learning to read how a new organization
actually works, and adapting quickly, came from the same habit — trying to
empathize with a different group of people every time.

---

## 1. Public Institutions — Making the Vast Legible at a Glance

The National Museum Foundation's online goods shop 'MU:DS' and the National
Folk Museum of Korea's main site both started from the same question: how do
you take an enormous, scattered archive and let people grasp it at a glance,
reaching what they came for, faster.

For MU:DS, I first looked at what people actually clicked on most, where they
dropped off, and what errors were showing up in customer-service tickets. The
problem was rarely a missing feature — more often, a feature existed but users
couldn't find it. Only after mapping that usage pattern did I redesign where
things lived, which made the shopping experience noticeably smoother.

For the National Folk Museum, the priority was untangling content that had
grown scattered over time into a menu structure people wouldn't get lost in.
I designed around what content mattered most to visitors and how it should be
categorized to match its nature — and because the visitor base skews toward
both children and older adults, I enlarged key content and simplified the
structure so anyone could focus without friction.

Neither project was about making things pretty. Both were about getting users
where they wanted to go, faster — and after each relaunch, customer
satisfaction rose noticeably.

## 2. Startups — Catching Repeated Questions and Drop-off Through Design

At Campus Fund, the nature of the work meant I moved fluidly between customer
support, marketing, and design. Sorting through recurring inquiries, I found
that "what is P2P investing?" came up more than anything else — alongside a
steady stream of questions about whether the company itself could be trusted.
I built a page that answered both at once: explaining how the investment model
worked, and visualizing the company's growth alongside it.

The result wasn't just fewer repeat questions — when new inquiries did come in,
sending that one link was often enough to close the loop without further
back-and-forth. Noticing what users were curious about, and what made them
anxious, translating that into an interface people could actually follow, and
watching that interface reduce the same conversation happening over and over —
it all turned out to be one continuous process.

## 3. Small Business — Client Communication for the Best Possible Fit

Small business work was never the easy option — if anything, the conversations
were more demanding. Working one-on-one with a client meant carefully
understanding the exact image they wanted to project to their customers, while
keeping usability front and center without losing the brand's sense of itself.
Budget-conscious clients, in particular, wanted to be able to manage and update
things themselves afterward — so getting the most out of a limited budget meant
correctly identifying what the client actually needed most, and acting on that
priority.

Jeon, a Korean restaurant in Vancouver, is the clearest example. The client
wanted an elevated, upscale feel, and I proposed gold paired with a deep,
refined traditional green as the accent color — a combination that resonated
enough that they rebuilt the entire site's tone around it. A small difference
in color can change a brand's whole impression, and this project confirmed
that again.

At the same time, what customers actually came to the site for was the menu.
I removed the friction of downloading a PDF, optimized it as a responsive page,
and structured the interaction around just three broad categories to keep it
simple. That menu page now sees over 150 visitors a day, and the site ranks
near the top when people search "downtown Korean food" on Google. The color
that shapes first impressions, and the usability of the screen people actually
use — within a limited budget, the work was about deciding which one mattered
more, and when.

## 4. Software — Organizing Vast Functionality Into a Legible Screen

Admin panels and dashboards were a different kind of problem. The person using
this screen was an internal user who logged in every day, and the goal wasn't
to look beautiful — it was to let them work through a large number of
functions without any cognitive overload.

Kangwon Land's admin console and the academic system for the Korea National
University of Arts (K-Arts) are two examples. As the number of functions grew,
grouping them into broad categories made more sense than listing them one by
one, and icons kept them from blurring together. The main screen needed to
surface stats and key functions at a glance, so the priority was a clear
information hierarchy over decoration. Organized, but never simple — that was
the goal for both.

The more repetitive a task, the fewer clicks it should take. The more
frequently a function is used, the more it should stand out. The same
principle that guides design for end users held true here as well.

---

## Conclusion

Whether it was a public institution's sprawling archive, a startup's repeated
questions, a small restaurant's menu, or the countless functions packed into
a daily-use admin screen — the scale and context changed every time, but what
I actually did stayed the same: understanding what the user needed, expressing
it in a form people could easily follow, and connecting the brand to the
person on the other side of the screen.

This is the work I want to keep doing — empathizing, expressing, and
connecting.
